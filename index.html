§ I  ·  The Idea

An alpine lake you cannot drive to.

Lake Lovely Water sits in a cirque on the west side of the Squamish River, a shelf of pale water held in a bowl of Tantalus granite. The peaks above it — Pelops, Niobe, and Tantalus Mountain — are the sort of shapes you point at from the highway and assume someone else gets to climb. The lake at their feet is small, cold, and clear enough that the bottom looks closer than it is.

There is no road. The trailhead is on the far bank of a fast, glacial river, which means every trip here begins with a water crossing — by canoe, by raft, by jet boat, by whatever you can responsibly get across in. From the west bank it is a steady 6.5 kilometres and roughly 1,300 metres up to the lake, through old growth and over slabs polished by ice that left a long time ago. Four to six hours, if you are fit and the day is on your side.

“You can see the Tantalus from the Sea to Sky Highway every day of your life and never once stand at the foot of it. Most people don’t. That’s the point of the place.” Field notes, late summer

It is a destination in the strict sense: nobody passes through Lake Lovely Water on the way to somewhere else. You go because you wanted to be there, and because you were willing to do what it asks of you.

§ II  ·  The Two Ways In

The river is the gate. Choose how you cross it.

There are essentially two ways to the west-bank trailhead. Neither is wrong. They are different trips for different parties.

Option One

Paddle the river yourself.

For experienced moving-water paddlers

Put in on the east bank near the mouth of Sigurd Creek, ferry across the Squamish River, find the trail on the far side, and climb. This is the traditional way in — the way people have done it since the hut went up. Done right, it is the more satisfying trip.

Done wrong, it is unforgiving. The Squamish runs fast and cold off the icefields. Sweepers along the banks have killed strong paddlers. If you have not paddled real moving water before, this is not the river to learn on.

A note on land: the trail begins on private property. Hikers are asked, as a courtesy, to contact the Sḵwx̱wú7mesh (Squamish) Nation band office before crossing.

Option Two

Take the jet boat across.

For most parties, most of the time

Squamish Jet Boat Journeys runs a chauffeured shuttle across the river to the west-bank trailhead. It removes the river from the equation: no ferry angles, no scout, no swim. You step off at the trail and start climbing.

This is the better choice for parties carrying heavy climbing gear, for sore knees, for partners who don’t love whitewater, and for anyone who hasn’t spent a season on moving water. It is also, frankly, what most parties going up for the hut should do.

Pickups can usually be arranged for the way out, so you don’t come down on the last day to find your boat gone.

§ III  ·  The Hut

Tantalus Hut, 1961.

The Alpine Club of Canada’s Vancouver Section built the hut in 1961, on a small bench above the lake. It sleeps sixteen, has a kitchen with mismatched mugs that have outlasted three generations of guidebook authors, a wood stove, propane burners, and a small dock with a single boat tied to it most summers.

Bookings are member-only and run through ACC Vancouver. The hut is booked whole — you and your party have it, or another party does — in three-night blocks running Friday to Sunday or Monday to Wednesday. That structure is part of what keeps the place quiet: there is no walk-up traffic, no day-trippers, no scramble for a bunk. Just whoever managed to put a group together and a calendar slot.

On a clear night the lake settles like glass and the granite goes pink, then grey, then dark. Inside, the stove is on, somebody is making pasta, and somebody else is reading the cabin log. It is a small, unfussy place, and people remember their nights there for the rest of their lives.

§ IV  ·  When To Go

A short window, honestly described.

The usable season is roughly six weeks. Snow lingers earlier than people think; weather closes the door earlier than people hope.

June — mid-July

Snow on the upper trail.

Lower switchbacks are mud and meltwater. The bowl above can hold snow well into July. Beautiful, and possible, with axe and crampons and a real forecast.

Late July — August

The honest window.

Trail is dry, water is open, peaks are climbable. Mid-week is calmer than the weekend. This is when to go.

First week of September

Quietest, sharpest light.

Bugs have given up, smoke usually clears, water is glassy in the mornings. Nights at the lake near freezing. Bring a warmer bag than you think you need.

Mid-September on

The door closes.

First serious system can put fresh snow on the peaks overnight. River levels swing on rainfall. Wait until next year unless you really know what you’re reading.

§ V  ·  The Pack

What goes in the bag.

Pack for a real alpine hut trip, not a campground weekend. The hut covers shelter and a stove; everything else is on you.

Worn & carried

  • i.Real boots. Stiff enough for talus. Broken in.
  • ii.Rain shell that has actually been waterproofed this season.
  • iii.Insulated mid-layer — even in August, even on a forecast day.
  • iv.Trekking poles. The descent is the part that breaks people.
  • v.50–65 L pack with a real hip belt.

In the hut

  • i.Sleeping bag rated lower than you’d guess — the hut gets cold once the stove dies.
  • ii.Inflatable pad or liner; bunks are wood.
  • iii.Indoor shoes or thick socks. The floor is cold and gritty.
  • iv.Headlamp with fresh batteries. Spare batteries.
  • v.Food for every meal, plus one extra dinner. No resupply.

For the lake & the ridge

  • i.Map & compass. A downloaded GPX is fine; rely on it alone at your risk.
  • ii.Sun hat, sunglasses, real sunscreen. The granite reflects everything back at you.
  • iii.Filter or chemical treatment. The lake is clear, not sterile.
  • iv.Light scrambling layer if you plan to wander up toward Pelops or Niobe.
  • v.Swim things. You will regret leaving them at home.

Safety & small things

  • i.Real first-aid kit, not a hotel-room one.
  • ii.Satellite messenger or PLB. There is no cell signal up there.
  • iii.Trip plan left with somebody who knows when to start worrying.
  • iv.Lighter and a spare. Matches in a dry bag.
  • v.Cash, in case the boat operator’s card reader is having a day.
§ VI  ·  Honest Talk

What this trip actually asks of you.

We don’t sell the mountains. They’ve done their own marketing for a long time. These are the things people get wrong.

i.

The river is the river.

The Squamish is fast, cold, and fed by ice. Sweepers along the banks have killed strong paddlers. If you are not a confident moving-water paddler, take the jet boat. Nobody at the hut will be impressed that you swam across; they’ll be relieved you didn’t.

ii.

The trail eats knees.

Six and a half kilometres and roughly 1,300 metres of gain is steep by any measure. Going up is hard. Going down with a hut-weight pack, on tired legs, on roots and slabs, is what hurts. Poles are not optional for anyone with knee history. Budget more time for the descent than you think.

iii.

Alpine weather makes its own plans.

The Tantalus catches systems coming off the coast and sits on them. A clear morning at the river can be a whiteout at the lake by mid-afternoon. Carry the layers. If the forecast goes sideways, the right answer is sometimes to stay put another night, not push out in cloud.

iv.

Bear country, the unromantic kind.

Black bears use these valleys. Cook well away from the hut, store food properly inside, pack out everything, and don’t leave snacks in your pack at the door. You will not see one most trips. That is the goal.

v.

No cell. Plan accordingly.

There is no reception at the hut and very little along the trail. Carry a satellite messenger or PLB, leave a written trip plan with a person who will call SAR if you are overdue, and agree in advance on what overdue means. Don’t assume someone else in the hut has a working device.

vi.

Leave it better than you found it.

The hut works because three generations of users have treated it like theirs. Sweep when you leave. Pack out micro-trash. Write something kind in the log. And if you are paddling across the river, contact the Sḵwx̱wú7mesh (Squamish) Nation band office first — the trail begins on their land, and a phone call is a small courtesy for the privilege.
